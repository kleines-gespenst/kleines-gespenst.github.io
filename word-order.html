EMNLP 2026 · Oral

Left-Branching Transformers Excel at Right-Branching Languages

Data Shapes Word Order Preferences in Language Models

Varvara Arzt, Allan Hanbury, and Terra Blevins

TU Wien · Northeastern University

Paper Code Slides

Oral presentation: 25 October 2026, 11:00–12:30, Session 2, room G15–16.

In short

Language models are used across languages with very different word orders. We ask four questions: Do LMs have systematic word order preferences? Are these preferences architectural or data-driven? Do they mirror patterns attested in natural languages? And do preferences on artificial languages match those on natural languages?

Prior work studies only one setting at a time: artificial languages are controlled but lack semantics, morphology and discourse, while in natural languages word order cannot be isolated. We run parallel experiments on both, with the same GPT-2 architecture and matched training data scales:

  • Artificial languages. 192 PCFG-generated languages (6 base word orders × 25 internal constituent orderings) with identical data that differ only in word order, and one shared word-boundary BPE tokeniser. We train GPT-2 small from scratch on each language at 5 and 10 MB and measure perplexity.
  • Natural languages. More than 70 typologically diverse languages, evaluated on FLORES-200 and PUD with BPEC (bits per English character, comparable across tokenisers). We focus on monolingual Goldfish models, which follow the GPT-2 architecture and were pretrained on 5, 10, 100 and 1000 MB per language, plus multilingual mGPT, XGLM and BLOOM.

The two settings point in opposite directions: on artificial languages, models consistently prefer left-branching; on natural languages, this bias is overridden by data at scale, and better-resourced, right-branching SVO languages become easiest.

Results

Takeaways

  1. Systematic, but not human-like. On artificial languages, LMs prefer left-branching regardless of base word order, and do not mirror the word order correlations found in natural languages.
  2. Data overrides the architectural bias. On natural languages at scale, better-resourced, right-branching SVO languages become easiest, in both monolingual and multilingual models.
  3. Artificial and natural results go in opposite directions. SVO is the hardest base word order on artificial languages and the easiest on natural ones at scale.

Even monolingual models trained on just 1 GB develop an SVO preference, and the best-resourced languages, such as English and Chinese, are predominantly SVO, so we expect frontier models to amplify this preference. Our results point to a precondition for silent typological homogenisation: especially languages with flexible word order may drift towards their dominant word order.
